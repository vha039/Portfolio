Deliverable

A shared benefits methodology for the whole hospital

There were four of us further developing an existing benefits methodology, commissioned by management. It was the Analytics Department's first large project. I contributed research synthesis, economic assessments, method development and governance, and I wrote all the VBA in the benefits database, one of the changes that was introduced. The methodology was adopted as the shared standard for the whole hospital and was still in use about two and a half years later.

Method development in a team of four

#

The work started with an existing methodology. The task was to improve it: a shared set of concepts and economic assessments that could be used across different measures.

Benefits can relate to resource use, capacity, quality or finances. The assessment must clarify what is expected to change, what assumptions the calculation rests on, and who can carry out the change. A sum alone does not give those answers.

Economic and methodological assessments

#

One database for calculation and follow-up

#

Calculation, checking and follow-up were brought together in a benefits database. Automated VBA calculated present value and checked the figures, and realisation could be followed up directly against plan.

When present value and checks are calculated in the same way for all measures, different benefit assessments can be compared without forcing the measures into the same template.

That requires the assumptions in each measure to be visible, so that they can be tested one by one.

Separating expected and realised benefit

#

A benefits plan often estimates freed capacity or financial effect before the change has been carried out.

In the revised methodology I contributed the distinction between expected and realised benefit. Saved time does not automatically reduce an expense, so expected effect, measures carried out and measured result must be kept separate.

The effect depends on what the freed capacity is used for and which changes are actually carried out. A combined net value without these distinctions can make a planned effect look like an achieved saving.

The assessment needs more information than an amount: assumptions, responsibilities and later observations.

Sources and limits

#

The internal methodology and the benefits database are not published.

  • The worked examples were written for this page, with fictitious measures.
  • The adoption of the methodology does not say how large the benefits from the measures have been.

Technical basis

#
Amounts need a price level, a year and a sign

An economic plan must describe what an amount refers to. Measure, year of effect, price level and type of effect are different pieces of information. Two amounts are not directly comparable just because both are in kroner.

The synthetic annual example uses explicit price factors and keeps expected benefit and investment apart. If a factor is missing, there is no calculated amount for that row. It cannot be replaced with zero.

The base amount is a nominal amount per year of effect. To compare the years in the chosen price year, the price year's factor is divided by the factor for the year of effect. The annual amount is rounded to the nearest øre, one hundredth of a krone, before the total is calculated. The factors are given example values.

Newly written example. It shows the pattern, not the original code.

// The same validated calculation is used in the static and the interactive view.
// The keys are the site's own parameter names:
// belop = amount, fra = first year, til = last year, prisaar = price year.
const plan = R8Calc.benefits({
  belop: 100000,
  type: "gevinst", // "gevinst" = benefit, "investering" = investment
  fra: 2026,
  til: 2030,
  prisaar: 2026
});
// Each row shows the year of effect, the relative price factor and the amount in the chosen price year.
// An investment gets a negative sign after the absolute amount is rounded.
When the arithmetic is right but the assessment remains

A model can add up correctly and still rest on a weak assumption. This applies, for example, to how much of the freed capacity can actually be used, and who is able to change resource use.

The allocation model for sterile logistics, which I designed as a separate project, answers a related question: how expected benefits can be distributed when direct measurement does not give a good enough basis.
